Spotting harmful coaching before it reaches a child.

The Youth Coach Behavioral Assessment (YCBA) is a structured behavioral screen that identifies high-risk coaching traits — grandiosity, callousness, aggression, and emotional volatility — so they can be reviewed before a coach is placed with young athletes.

What it does

A structured screen, not a gut feeling

Harmful coaches are often charming on the surface. The YCBA replaces intuition with a validated, facet-level behavioral profile that routes concerns to a trained reviewer.

🛡️

Prevention-first

Designed to sit at the licensing or onboarding stage — a check that happens before a coach is ever alone with athletes.

📊

Evidence-based items

Every item is adapted from a published psychological instrument, mapped to a recognized behavioral construct.

⚖️

Human-in-the-loop

An elevated result never decides an outcome. It flags a profile for structured, fair review — never an automatic label.

Five domains

What the YCBA measures

Grandiose Narcissism

Superiority, entitlement, exploitation, and a need for admiration that eclipses athlete welfare.

Callous / Antisocial

Lack of remorse, deceit, rule-breaking, and impulsivity — the markers of exploitative conduct.

Anger & Aggression

Poorly controlled temper, verbal and physical aggression, and a hostile outlook.

Emotional Volatility

Rapid mood shifts and unstable, controlling attachment to individual athletes.

Response Validity

A built-in check for defensive, overly favorable answering that could mask real risk.

The Assessment

How the YCBA is built

A 39-item self-report form across five scales. Each scale breaks into named facets drawn directly from the measures clinicians and researchers already use.

Ready to see your profile?

The assessment takes about 12–15 minutes and scores instantly, entirely in your browser.

The Science

The evidence behind the items

The YCBA does not invent new psychology. It assembles validated constructs from decades of personality and sport-psychology research and translates them into the coaching context.

Source instruments

Where each scale comes from

Scale A · Narcissism

Five-Factor Narcissism Inventory (FFNI)

A Five-Factor-Model measure distinguishing grandiose from vulnerable narcissism across facets such as entitlement, exploitativeness, and need for admiration.

Scale B · Antisocial

Levenson Self-Report Psychopathy Scale (LSRP)

Separates primary features (callousness, manipulation) from secondary features (impulsivity, rule-breaking) in non-institutional samples.

Scale C · Aggression

Buss–Perry Aggression Questionnaire & STAXI

Established measures of trait aggression across physical aggression, verbal aggression, anger, and hostility sub-scales.

Scale D · Volatility

MSI-BPD & Controlling Coach Behaviours Scale

A brief borderline-features screen paired with a self-determination-theory measure of controlling coaching (intimidation, conditional regard, excessive control).

Scale V · Validity

Balanced Inventory of Desirable Responding (BIDR)

Impression-management items detect a defensive "fake-good" response style, so trait scores can be read with the right caution.

Method

Facet-level scoring

Responses are averaged (1–5) at the facet and scale level, then banded Low / Moderate / Elevated against illustrative thresholds.

From response to review

How a profile is used

1

Candid self-report

The coach rates 39 statements on how characteristic each is of them across a full season.

2

Facet & validity scoring

Each domain is scored by facet, with an impression-management index that flags under-reporting.

3

Banded profile

Scores are banded and summarized — reassuring where low, attention-directing where elevated.

4

Structured human review

Elevated profiles route to a trained reviewer alongside observer reports and context. The screen never decides alone.

Ethics & governance. In a live deployment, responses would be collected under informed consent, stored securely, governed by an approved IRB protocol, and released only to trained reviewers with due process.
Before you beginIntroduction
Youth Coach Behavioral Assessment

Self-Report Form (YCBA-SR · v1.0)

The statements ahead describe thoughts, feelings, and behaviors a coach might have. For each one, indicate how characteristic or true it is of you as a coach. There are no right answers — the screen only works when responses are candid.

  • Rate your typical self across a full season, not one good or bad day.
  • Answer every item; you can move back and forth between sections.
  • Your responses are scored locally and are never transmitted or stored.
Prototype only. Not a validated instrument; not for any real hiring, licensure, disciplinary, or clinical decision.

References & Disclosure

Instruments & further reading

The YCBA adapts item content and structure from published descriptions of the following measures.

  • FFNI — Glover, N., et al. (2012). The Five-Factor Narcissism Inventory: A five-factor measure of narcissistic personality traits. Journal of Personality Assessment.
  • LSRP — Levenson, M. R., Kiehl, K. A., & Fitzpatrick, C. M. (1995). Assessing psychopathic attributes in a noninstitutionalized population. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology.
  • Buss–Perry AQ — Buss, A. H., & Perry, M. (1992). The Aggression Questionnaire. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology.
  • STAXI — Spielberger, C. D. (1999). State-Trait Anger Expression Inventory-2.
  • MSI-BPD — Zanarini, M. C., et al. (2003). A screening measure for BPD: The McLean Screening Instrument for Borderline Personality Disorder. Journal of Personality Disorders.
  • Controlling Coach Behaviours Scale — Bartholomew, K. J., Ntoumanis, N., & Thøgersen-Ntoumani, C. (2010). The controlling interpersonal style in a coaching context. Journal of Sport & Exercise Psychology.
  • BIDR — Paulhus, D. L. (1991). Balanced Inventory of Desirable Responding.
Disclosure. This is an independent research prototype by AreyTech. It is not affiliated with, endorsed by, or validated against any of the instruments above, and must not be used for applied screening decisions.
